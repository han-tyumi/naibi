## `conquian` — ten

The entry describes the two-player Anglo-American game. **That was checked
before it was corrected and it was left alone**: pagat leads with the Mexican
game, at two or three players with eight cards each, and treats the two-hander
as the Anglo-American form; Wikipedia's own rules section is the two-hander,
ten cards and eleven melded. The sources genuinely differ about which is the
game, and following Wikipedia's is a defensible choice, not an error. What was
wrong was everything the entry said about the other one.

Main rules:

1. **A sequence had no maximum.** The entry allowed "three or more cards of one
   suit". Both sources cap it at eight, and pagat gives the reason: eleven
   melded wins, eleven cannot be one combination, and whatever a long sequence
   is paired with still needs three cards.
2. **The deal was called traditional when the sources do not agree what it is.**
   "Traditionally in packets of two" — Wikipedia deals five packets of two,
   pagat deals one at a time, and neither calls either the tradition.
3. **Forcing carried an invented deadline.** The entry said the opponent "must
   say so before you turn the next stock card". Neither source puts any timing
   on it. Deleted, and pagat's actual extra rule added in its place: the player
   doing the forcing may rearrange the victim's melds to make the card fit.
4. **The stake on a draw was upgraded.** "Plenty of players double it" against
   pagat's "some play".
5. **The scoring for a session without money ran the wrong way.** The entry had
   the loser accumulating the cards they were left holding, low score winning.
   Wikipedia awards those cards to the winner and plays to an agreed total, and
   gives a value schedule for the short pack: two to seven at face value, ten a
   court, fifteen an ace. The entry's own alternative — a point a deal and a
   race to five or seven — is in neither source.

Variants:

6. **The multi-player game used the wrong pack.** "Shuffle two 40-card packs
   together" — one pack serves. The numbers our variant carried are Navajo Tens',
   which is a different game on the same page.
7. **And the wrong deal and target.** It dealt ten and set the goal at eleven.
   Three players take eight each and go out on nine; Wikipedia adds four players
   at seven and eight. Both sources agree on the three-player figures.
8. **Panguingue was given five to eight players**; pagat says four to eight.
9. **Panguingue was called "Conquian's gambling descendant".** pagat's Conquian
   page calls it a possible distant relative of Paco, and both its own page and
   Wikipedia's put its origin in the Philippines rather than Mexico.
10. **Both deck-size variants claimed an effect on how often deals are drawn** —
    "draws become a little less common", "draws become rare" — which neither
    source states. Removed, and pagat's actual note about the long pack added in
    its place: it is very unusual, and the short pack is what players use.

## `dou-dizhu` — four

This was the strongest of the three, and its thirteen combinations, its
scoring and its four-player variant are all exact against pagat line by line.

1. **The base text asserted a rule its own variant contradicts.** `setup` had
   the landlord picking up "three extra cards nobody else has seen", while the
   variants block says turning them face up has become normal. Wikipedia's rules
   section simply reveals them. The claim to secrecy is gone from `setup`; which
   way a table does it stays in the variant, where it belongs.
2. **"Standard in most online rooms"**, of that reveal. Wikipedia's sentence
   about online rooms is about who bids first, not about the three cards.
   Nothing ranks the reveal.
3. **"A common house rule"**, of the three-of-hearts opening bid. Wikipedia says
   "some house rules".
4. **The bomb figure's note was false.** "Beats all but a rocket" — a bomb gives
   way to a bigger bomb too, which the prose says correctly.

**Recorded and not acted on.** The two sources give different combination lists
for the four-player game: pagat has bombs of four cards or more with more cards
beating fewer and a rocket of all four jokers, while Wikipedia names six, seven
and eight of a kind separately and calls four jokers a nuke. The entry follows
pagat, whose list is complete and consistent with the payment rule the entry
also carries; Wikipedia's is a bare list with no play or payment rules attached.
This is a disagreement left standing, not a verified fact.

## `piquet` — five

1. **A disputed rule was stated flatly, and the undisputed half of it was
   missing.** The entry said "the 10 for the cards cannot be used to reach the
   thirty" needed for a pique. pagat says exactly that; Wikipedia states the
   opposite as the rule, that elder taking a seventh trick before younger has
   any may apply the 10 to a pique at once. What **both** sources agree on is
   that the 40 for capot never counts, and the entry did not say it. It now says
   the capot rule plainly and sends the disputed one to the variants.
2. **The same disagreement was miscast in the variants.** "Claiming the cards
   early" gave one reading as a house rule and the other as "the standard laws",
   when the two published accounts simply differ. Rewritten to say so and to
   tell a table to settle it first.
3. **"It is common enough to be worth asking about."** pagat says "some people
   allow" it; Wikipedia gives it as the rule. Neither says how common the house
   rule is.
4. **"One of the two or three best card games ever devised for exactly two
   people."** pagat says one of the best and most skilful for two; Parlett, via
   Wikipedia, calls it one of the most skill-rewarding for two and adds that it
   is now played only by aficionados. Nobody ranks it in a top three. Reworded,
   and Parlett's second half — which changes what a reader should expect at a
   table — added.
5. **The sequence figure over-claimed.** "The step from four cards to five is
   the sharpest jump in the game": capot over the cards is a jump of thirty. It
   is the sharpest rise on that ladder, and now says so.

**Not acted on, recorded.** Wikipedia carries two things the entry has no room
for and does not contradict: a carte rouge bonus, and a rule that a player
scores repique or pique but never both. Neither is in pagat. Wikipedia also
files examining the opponent's tricks as a variation where pagat gives it as
the rule; the entry follows pagat.

## The originality pass, run after the fact fixes

**Ten passes running, every REUSE finding has been introduced by that day's own
correction, and this pass did not break the run.** One appeared, and it was the
last sentence written before the run: `piquet`'s background had been reworded to
add Parlett's point that the game now belongs to enthusiasts, and it came out
carrying eight words of Wikipedia — "from the end of the First World War" and
what sat either side of it. It says 1918 now.

That was not the only passage rewritten for wording. Four were, all four on the
evidence of the sentence pairs behind an ORDER finding rather than on the
finding's own say-so, and one is the useful part of this section:

- `conquian`'s corrected draw sentence had picked up Wikipedia's "the stake
  carried forward" while the fix was being written. Reworded.
- `conquian`'s deal sentence walked Wikipedia's clause order — ten cards each,
  five packets of two, remainder face down as stock. Restructured to define the
  stock by what was not dealt.
- `piquet`'s cut-for-the-deal sentence was a synonym swap of pagat's "a slight
  advantage to being non-dealer on the critical sixth hand". **The first rewrite
  moved it from 43% aligned against Wikipedia to 71%**: writing away from one
  source landed it on the other, which had said the same thing in a different
  shape. The second rewrite inverts the deduction — the edge first, the cut last
  — and sits at 43% again against a sentence it no longer echoes.

The two piquet cases are the same lesson twice in one entry, and it is sharper
than the nine passes before it: a correction written next to a source copies the
source, and a correction written to *escape* a source copies the other one.
Neither passage was wrong on the facts. Both were rewritten because of where the
sentence had been written, not because of what it said.

What was judged and left: `dou-dizhu`'s "four cards of the same rank", the
definition of a bomb and unrewordable; `piquet`'s dealer-is-younger-hand and
carte blanche definitions, which are the naming of terms of art; and `piquet`'s
twelve-cards-each deal, which is the formulaic procedure both sources and this
project's own kept list already treat as having one form. The remaining
whole-passage alignments sit at the bar itself, 75%, and their sentence pairs
were read one by one: most pair our sentences against source sentences about
unrelated things, which is what that heuristic looks like when it fires on
noise. The 2026-08-05 record said the same of `conquian`'s, and it is still true.

**The tool was controlled before it was trusted.** A sentence copied verbatim
from the pagat page on disk was planted in `conquian` and the run reported it as
nine words verbatim, against the right source, with both sides printed; it was
then removed. Fetching was controlled in both directions first: real pages
answered 200 and invented ones 404 at both pagat and Wikipedia, and Wikipedia
was read through its API, which returns an explicit `missing` marker rather than
a page titled with whatever was asked for. pagat's suit images survive as their
`alt` text — 26 of them in the Conquian page, 24 in Piquet — so no suit was
silently dropped.
