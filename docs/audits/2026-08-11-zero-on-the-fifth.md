here rather than opening two more entries is the handoff's instruction being
followed, not a shortfall against it.

## What a third source bought

The two previous 2026-08-11 sittings each read two sources. This one read three,
and **five of the fifteen findings are only visible with all three open**:

- The base game's most-quoted rule, what "Mau-Mau" means, splits **two against
  one**, and the entry had followed the minority.
- The voluntary draw splits **two against one** the other way, and the entry had
  followed the minority there too.
- The seven splits **two against one**, and the entry had followed the majority
  but stated it flatly.
- The ace is described three different ways by three sources, which is only
  visible as a pattern at three.
- The origin date splits, and neither side is reported by the other.

That is the strongest argument this audit has produced for the range in
CONTRIBUTING's "two or three independent sources". At two, four of those five
look like settled facts.

## `mau-mau` — fifteen

The announcements and the draw:

1. **The rule the game is named after was the minority version, stated flatly.**
   The entry had "Mau" on the second-to-last card and "Mau-Mau" on the last.
   English Wikipedia and Game Rules both reserve "Mau-Mau" for **going out on a
   jack**, with nothing said over an ordinary last card; only the German article
   has it on any last card. Now says the first part plainly and the second as
   the split it is.
2. **The voluntary draw was demoted to "a few groups".** Game Rules and the
   German article both make it the base rule, in as many words: a player who
   *can* play may draw instead, and may decline the card they draw. Only English
   Wikipedia is silent. Now the base rule, with the strict version noted.
3. **"Most German households do not permit a jack to be laid on a jack."**
   Nothing ranks this, and both sources that define the jack say it plays on any
   card at all. Reversed, with the restriction kept as the house rule it is.

The power cards:

4. **The seven's forfeited turn was stated flatly.** Game Rules has the next
   player draw two and pass; English Wikipedia has them draw two **but may
   play**. Now says the turn is the disputed part.
5. **"Both are widespread"**, of the two readings of the ace — and there are
   three. English Wikipedia has a second card played along with it, the Czech
   and Russian variants have the next player skipped, and Game Rules gives the
   ace no power at all. Rewritten to give the three and rank none.
6. **"A number of groups refuse to let you go out on a seven or a jack."**
   Going out on a jack is exactly what the second announcement is for, in both
   sources that mention it, and English Wikipedia doubles the score for it. What
   *is* barred, where anything is, is a finish on an ace or on any card with a
   power. Corrected.

The opening card:

7. **"Bury any jack that appears there back in the stock."** In no source. The
   German article gives two other remedies — the dealer names the suit, or the
   bottom card of the stock decides — and those are what the entry says now.

Scoring:

8. **"100 and 150 are both common choices."** 150 is the only figure any of the
   three prints. The 100 and the ranking are gone.
9. **"A simplified set of values is nearly as common as the traditional one."**
   Nothing ranks them, and the ranking is the wrong way round if anything: the
   "simplified" set is the only one a source read actually gives.

Variants:

10. **The Austrian variant was named `Neunerln`.** The one source that covers it
    calls it **Neuner**. Renamed.
11. **"The jack becomes an ordinary card"** in that variant. The source says
    nines are added as wild cards; it does not say the jack stops being one.
12. **Tschau Sepp's two-part call was invented.** The entry had "Tschau" on the
    second-to-last card and "Sepp" on the last. The German article has
    "Tschau Sepp" as the single shout on going out, in place of "Mau-Mau".
13. **Three more Tschau Sepp claims rest on nothing read** — that it uses Jass
    cards, that the under is the card which changes suit, and that it is one of
    the best-known games in Switzerland and often the first children learn.
    Replaced with what the sources do carry: a 36-card pack, on record since the
    early 1960s, and the Swiss habit of counting the losers' cards.
14. **"The single most common point of disagreement"**, of stacking sevens.
    Nothing ranks it against the ace, which is messier.
15. **"The other special cards, in the set most commonly played"**, introducing
    the list of powers — a ranking undercut by the entry's very next paragraph,
    which tells you every family has its own list. Caught by reading the
    rendered page rather than the diff, which is the second sitting running that
    the rendered page has found something the diff did not.

**Recorded, not acted on.** The `scoring_table`'s values — ace 11, ten 10, king
4, queen 3, jack 20 — are supported by **none of the three sources**. The German
article says only that values are borrowed from other games, which fits the Skat
-like look of the first four, but the jack at 20 belongs to the flatter scheme
Game Rules prints and the combination appears nowhere. It has not been deleted,
because nothing read contradicts it either; the prose no longer presents it as
the traditional set against a simplified one, and says plainly that values are
not standardised. **This is the first table in forty-six audited entries with a
question over it, and the question is provenance rather than arithmetic.**

## The originality pass, run after the fact fixes

**Eleven passes running, and again the only finding came from that day's own
correction.** The rewritten `background` had swapped "appeared" for Wikipedia's
"surfaced" — a single word, taken while their sentence was on screen, out of a
sentence that had been fine before it was touched. Reworded and re-run clean.

Two of the last three sittings have now produced a finding in a `background`
rewritten to add a historical detail, which is a narrow enough pattern to name:
history is the part of an entry where the source sentence and ours carry the
same handful of proper nouns and dates, so there is very little room left to be
different in.

**A false clean, and how it was caught.** Re-fetching the sources for the final
re-check ran into Wikipedia's rate limiter, which answers with a plain-text
apology rather than JSON. The extractor died on it, the shell redirect had
already truncated the file, and `wikipedia.txt` survived as a two-line stub. The
originality run then reported **`ok mau-mau (2 sources)`** and the stamp went
through — because two files existed and one of them had nothing in it to match
against. Nothing in that output distinguishes "compared against the article and
found nothing" from "compared against an empty file".

It was caught by checking the byte counts against the earlier fetch, and put
right by retrying with backoff until the file came back at 19,470 bytes with
both articles' titles in it — byte-identical to the first fetch, which is the
reproducibility check as well. The run was then controlled in the direction that
matters, by planting a sentence from the English article and confirming the
checker found it, before being re-run and re-stamped for real.

The general point is the project's own: **a report that can come back empty must
say what it did not look at.** `--stamp` refuses a check with fewer than two
sources, which is the guard that exists; it has no way to refuse a source file
that is present and empty, and this is the first time that gap has been hit.
