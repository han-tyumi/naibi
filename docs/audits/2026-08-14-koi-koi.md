## The shape of the trouble

Nearly every finding is the same mistake made repeatedly: **the entry demoted
things its sources state as rules, and promoted things they state as options.**
Five separate base rules were filed as house rules or as "optional", and two house
rules were stated as the way the game is played. The scoring section carried the
worst of it, because a demotion there changes what a hand is worth.

## `koi-koi` — twenty-two

**The sake cup, which is four findings in one card.**

1. **A base rule in both accounts was filed as a variant, and inverted on the way
   down.** The entry's variants block offered "let it count as a plain instead, at
   the holder's choice, which quietly boosts the ten-plain count". Both accounts
   say the sake cup counts as a plain **as well as** an animal, always and by
   default — one puts it in the ten-plain yaku's own definition, the other says the
   card "counts as both at the same time". So the entry filed the standard rule as
   an optional one, made it exclusive where it is cumulative, and described a
   restriction as a boost.
2. **The entry's own census could not see it.** `goal_and_scoring` listed the sake
   cup as earning "its keep in two viewing combinations and the animal count", three
   uses where the sources give four.
3. **"The chrysanthemum sake cup is the most argued-over card in the pack."** A
   superlative nothing ranks. What one account says is that the card is overpowered
   and that many house rules exist to curb it, which is what the entry now says.
4. **"Japanese tables frequently ban them outright."** Nothing counts Japanese
   tables. One account says video-game adaptations commonly offer the viewing yaku
   as a toggle, which is a different claim about a different population.

**The scoring structure, where the accounts genuinely differ.**

5. **"The exception is the brights, which are graded rather than cumulative."** The
   entry had grading applying to the brights alone. One account grades the ribbon
   and animal yaku the same way — best in each group only — and says this is typical
   of modern forms, Nintendo's current leaflet and video-game versions. The entry
   now gives both systems and names the difference instead of asserting one.
6. **Gokō was valued at 10.** The only account that ranks the values calls **15**
   the most common and names 10 as Nintendo's. Changed, with the alternative in the
   note.
7. **Sankō was valued at 5.** Same account calls **6** the most common and 5
   Nintendo's. Changed the same way.
8. **Ino-shika-chō stacking with tane was stated flatly.** Under the grouped system
   they are mutually exclusive, and ino-shika-chō itself grows by a point per extra
   animal instead.
9. **"Akatan and aotan together — simply 5 + 5; they stack."** Under the grouped
   system that pair is a single combination worth 10 which excludes the five-ribbon
   yaku rather than adding to it.

**Rules demoted to options.**

10. **The dealer's privilege was called an add-on.** "Some tables pay the dealer a
    point or two… that is an add-on rather than the base rule." One account makes it
    the rule outright, names it — oya-ken — and puts it at a point; the other files
    it as a house rule. The entry picked the second and told the reader the first
    does not exist.
11. **"A point or two" is not either account's figure.** The documented payments are
    **one point or six**.
12. **The lucky hands were called "an optional pair of instant wins".** Both accounts
    give teshi and kuttsuki as standard, and one says explicitly that these are the
    only two.
13. **"In most rule sets the winner of a round deals the next one."** Both accounts
    state it flatly.
14. **"Most rule sets therefore scrap the deal"** on a four-of-a-month field. Both
    state the misdeal flatly.
15. **"The version most widely used is that your call doubles your opponent's
    score."** Both accounts give exactly this as the rule, with no hedge to inherit.

**Options promoted to rules, and one that was already the rule.**

16. **"A gentler version caps all multipliers at a single doubling however many calls
    are made."** That is the base rule in both accounts — one says outright that the
    number of calls has no effect. The entry filed the standard behaviour as a
    house-rule softening.
17. **The cumulative multiplier was invented as a power of two.** "A second and third
    call push the stakes to four and eight times." The only account that records
    scaling gives the multiplier as **one more than the number of calls** — so a
    second call pays three times and a third four, not four and eight.
18. **"Many tables also double the caller's own score if the call comes good."** The
    account that records this gives it as a **replacement** for the opponent-called
    rule — the multiplier goes to the caller *instead of* their opponent — not as a
    second doubling stacked on top.

**The rest.**

19. **"It is the most variable rule in the game."** A superlative about which rule
    varies most, which nothing measures.
20. **"Or halve them"** — a way of reducing the viewing yaku that neither account
    records. The two documented reductions are to drop them, or to require another
    yaku alongside.
21. **"The cards are locked together and can only be shed in matched pairs."** The
    entry's own mechanism for why lucky hands are bad. The account says only that
    they are poor for capturing and that the points are compensation.
22. **Two apportionments nothing measures**: "choosing well is most of the ordinary
    skill in the game", and playing for a stake per point being "the traditional
    way".

**Twelve rules, yaku and variations the sources give and the entry lacked.**
**Tsukifuda**, all four cards of the round's own month, worth 4 and used mainly in
twelve-round games, was absent from the table entirely. **You are not obliged to
play a matching card** — one account says so outright, and it is a real strategic
option; the other's wording reads the other way, so the entry now names the
disagreement. **Four separate pairs dealt to the field is a misdeal too**, where the
entry covered only four of a month. **Both players holding a lucky hand is a draw**,
and the accounts differ on a player holding both at once — six points or twelve.
**Playing to fifty points** is a third game length. **A tie in the first-dealer draw**
goes to the higher-value card. **The deal may be made in packets of two** as well as
four. Also added: the **weather ruling** under which willow cards in the field rain
off the blossom viewing and paulownia cards fog out the moon viewing; the
**field multiplier** borrowed from Hachi-Hachi; the alternatives to a
four-of-a-month misdeal; the draw-round alternatives where the deal passes or the
round goes to whoever last improved a yaku; and the variation treating both lucky
hands as a misdeal.

**What came through clean.** The pack — 48 cards, twelve months of four, five
brights, nine animals, ten ribbons, twenty-four plains — the month being the only
thing that matches, the eight-eight-eight deal and the twenty-four-card draw pile,
the two-halves turn, the fourth card taking all four, the choice of which of two
matches to take, the captures being public, shōbu and koi-koi and what each does,
the dead round scoring nothing, twelve rounds for twelve months, the seven-point
doubling, the two multipliers compounding, and the worked thirty-six for a
nine-point hand against a caller — all correct. **So is the entry's own arithmetic
about the draw pile**, which is in neither source: eight cards each means sixteen
draws and leaves eight cards face down, so the round is bounded by the hands rather
than the pile. That reasoning holds.

## Recorded, not acted on

- **The yaku value changes rest on one account.** Gokō at 15 and sankō at 6 are
  changed on the strength of the single source that says which values are common,
  with Nintendo's 10 and 5 in the notes. The other source could not corroborate,
  because its table does not survive the extract. **This is the finding in the
  sitting most worth a third reader.**
- **The entry presents the stacking system in its table and the grouped system in
  its prose.** That is deliberate — the table has to pick one shape and the prose
  names both — but it is not ideal, and a third source would say which belongs in
  the table.
- **`Koi Koi` and `Hanafuda Koi-Koi` are unconfirmed aliases.** Neither account
  gives either as a name; both are spelling variants rather than names anyone
  records. Alias sweep.
- **Multiplayer Koi-Koi exists** and the entry is fixed at two players. One account
  mentions a multiplayer form in passing, with players paired across the table, and
  gives no rules for it. Not enough read to write a variant from, and unlike the
  `players.max` pattern the facet and the prose do not contradict each other here —
  the entry claims nothing about more than two.
- **The November lightning card as a wild card** is a documented variation that was
  left out. It needs the rules of another game to state correctly, which is what the
  source says.

## What this pass does not establish

The two accounts disagree about the shape of the scoring, the dealer's privilege,
and whether a player holding a match must use it. Each is now reported impersonally
in the entry, and none is settled. The entry's own note that a good ribbon hand is
worth roughly twice as much under one system as the other is arithmetic on the two
tables, not a claim either source makes.

The originality tool cannot certify the entry clean. What this sitting can say is
that its blind spot was worked around: the exhaustive longest-run sweep found a run
in a variant description the tool does not read, in prose written an hour earlier,
and the tool's own report for the entry named none of it.
