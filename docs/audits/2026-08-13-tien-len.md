## The pairing, which is where the biggest finding came from

`tien-len` was read against `dou-dizhu` (audited 2026-08-11) and `big-two`
(2026-08-09), its two climbing-game neighbours. The comparison produced the
sitting's most serious finding and pointed at where it came from.

**`dou-dizhu`'s entry says "Twos and jokers are barred from every sequence in the
game."** That is correct for Dou Dizhu. `tien-len` carried the same rule — "Twos
sit outside sequences altogether in the standard game" — and for Tien Len it is
wrong: **both published accounts give a two capping a run as the ordinary rule**,
one with the worked example J-Q-K-A-2 and the other with K♣ A♦ 2♣. The entry had
the real rule filed as a regional variant.

The same pattern shows in the play direction. `dou-dizhu` is anti-clockwise and
`big-two` is anticlockwise; both are right. `tien-len` said "Vietnamese players
usually pass the turn counter-clockwise" and **both its sources say clockwise**.

That is the `tripeaks`-described-Golf shape from the handoff, twice in one entry,
and neither instance is visible from inside `tien-len`. **Read the neighbour's
entry, not just your own sources.**

One thing the pairing established as correct and worth naming, because a careless
sweep would "fix" it: **`big-two` and `tien-len` genuinely disagree about suit
order.** `big-two` runs spades over hearts over clubs over diamonds, lowest card
3♦, highest 2♠; `tien-len` runs hearts, diamonds, clubs, spades, lowest 3♠,
highest 2♥. Both match their own sources. They are not meant to agree.

## `tien-len` — twelve

1. **The play direction was wrong, with a prevalence claim holding it up.**
   "Vietnamese players usually pass the turn counter-clockwise." Both sources say
   deal and play are clockwise; one adds that a table may agree to reverse it in
   advance. Nothing counts what Vietnamese players usually do. Direction is the
   third error category in the handoff and this is the plainest instance the audit
   has had.
2. **Who deals next was wrong.** "The deal then moves one seat along in the
   direction of play at the end of each hand." Both sources: **the loser of the
   previous hand deals.** One of them ties it to the same idea in its friendly
   form — the loser shuffles for the next match — so the two halves corroborate
   each other.
3. **A variant was the base rule and the base rule was a variant.** The entry:
   "Runs and chains climb from the three up to the ace and stop there. Twos sit
   outside sequences altogether in the standard game… neither is Q-K-A-2." Both
   sources give the two at the top of a run flatly. The rule the entry stated is
   the American form's, which one source names as Viet Cong and which — a detail
   the entry could not have had, since it did not know this was the variant —
   **bars twos only from single sequences and still allows them inside chains of
   pairs.** The entry now gives the two capping a run as the rule and the barring
   as the variation, renamed accordingly. **The figure caption carried the same
   error in five words** — "stop at the ace" — and the figure's first row was
   replaced with J♠ Q♦ K♣ A♥ 2♠ so the drawing now teaches the corrected rule
   rather than the old one.
4. **A prevalence claim propping up finding 3.** "Northern practice is stricter and
   keeps twos out of sequences entirely, **which is also the more widely published
   rule**." Both published accounts read here say the opposite. This is the clearest
   case the audit has found of a prevalence marker being the thing that made a
   wrong rule look settled.
5. **Invented geography.** "Some **southern Vietnamese** tables let a two cap a
   sequence… **Northern practice** is stricter." Neither source mentions northern or
   southern practice at all. The forms are real; the regions are not sourced.
6. **The opening-lead requirement was demoted to a house rule.** "Many groups
   require the opening play to actually contain it; others let that player lead
   whatever they please." Both sources require the holder of the lowest card to
   play it, and differ only on whether it may go down inside a combination or must
   be led alone. The entry now states the requirement and names both the
   difference and the two variations that loosen it — including the one that lets
   the holder pass, whose stated reason is that the three may be part of a chop.
7. **An invented ranking between the two chop types.** "Between those two, the four
   of a kind is the stronger, so it also beats a chain of three consecutive
   pairs." Neither source ranks them against each other, and one says the opposite
   in general terms: **once a chop is played, its shape becomes the shape everyone
   else must match.** Under that rule a four of a kind cannot be dropped on a chain
   of pairs at all.
8. **The settlement pointed the wrong way, with a prevalence marker on it.** "What
   people actually record is the order of finish, and **in most circles** a
   settlement based on how much you were still holding… The **common measure** is
   one unit for every card left in hand, paid by each player to the one who went
   out first." Both sources' base rule is the reverse: play runs until one player
   is left holding cards, **that player is the loser, and the loser pays a fixed
   stake to each of the others** — nothing counted card by card. The per-card
   scheme the entry gave as the common measure is one contributor's version in one
   source. Both are now given, in the right order, with the difference named.
9. **"This is where tables differ most."** A superlative about which part of the
   game varies most, which nothing measures.
10. **Two more prevalence markers on the chop schedule.** "A **common alternative**
    sets the price of a pair of twos at five consecutive pairs; **a few tables**
    also accept two fours of a kind in consecutive ranks." Both are the American
    form, given in both sources as that form's rules rather than ranked for
    frequency.
11. **"Unlike most Western games the ranking is used constantly."** Neither source
    compares Tien Len to Western games, and the corpus argues against it — `big-two`
    uses its suit ranking in every comparison too. Replaced with what the ranking
    does.
12. **The chop lengths were stated as exact where both sources say "or more".** "A
    pair of twos falls only to a chain of four consecutive pairs, and three twos
    only to a chain of five." Both sources give four-or-more and five-or-more, so a
    five-pair chain answers a pair of twos as well. The word "only" made a legal
    play illegal.

**Five rules and variants the sources give and the entry lacked.** A **passed
player may still play a chop** in one source's base rules, where the entry stated
the lock-out absolutely — that changes what a two is worth. **Three players may
take seventeen cards each** instead of thirteen, leaving one card over. **Five to
eight can play with two packs shuffled together.** **Trading** exists in two
documented forms — mutual exchange by agreement, and a President-style handicap on
the previous hand's placings — and the entry had neither; it is now a variant, and
it interacts with the instant-win rule, since a table that trades drops the
automatic win for four twos. And **one source lets a plain four of a kind take a
pair of twos** where the other does not, which the entry now says rather than
choosing.

**The arithmetic and the shapes came through clean.** The six legal plays, thirteen
cards each, the rank order with the two on top, the suit order, the 3♠ as the
lowest card and 2♥ as the highest, rank beating suit, comparison by the highest
card in the play, the worked example of two runs of four settling on their fourth
card, the pass-locks-you-out rule, the skipping of players who have gone out, and
the seventeen-each three-handed deal leaving exactly one card over — all correct.
So is the entry's own explanation of *why* the game needs chops at all, which is in
neither source: the 2♥ cannot be beaten by any ordinary single, so without chops it
would be unanswerable. That reasoning holds.
