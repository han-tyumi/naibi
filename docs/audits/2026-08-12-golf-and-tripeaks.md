## Why these two

They are the same move. Golf and TriPeaks both ask you to take an exposed card
one rank from the top of a single waste pile, ignoring suit, and both count the
game over when the stock runs out — Wikipedia's TriPeaks article opens by calling
it "akin to the solitaire games Golf and Black Hole", and each of our entries
carries a variant describing the other. Whether the two agree cannot be checked
from inside either one. `golf-multiplayer` was read at the same time for the same
reason: it also describes the patience, in a paragraph written to keep readers
from confusing the two.

**That is where the sixteenth finding came from, and it is the whole argument
for pairing entries.** `tripeaks`'s Golf variant said "Traditional Golf does not
wrap at the ace" — true, and exactly as far as our own `golf` entry went before
this sitting. But `golf`'s strict form, corrected an hour earlier on the same
day, has a second restriction: nothing at all may be packed on a king, not even
a queen. The neighbouring entry was still describing the pre-audit version of a
game audited the same morning. It now names both stops. **`golf-multiplayer`'s
paragraph came back clean** — "seven columns of five", "one rank up or down", and
"waste" rather than "foundation", which is the naming choice `golf` discloses in
its own setup.

## `golf` — nine

**The flagship is a variation promoted to the base rule, with the real rule in
the entry's own hedge** — the shape this audit was set up to find, and one it has
now turned up in several sittings running.

1. **The king rule was inverted.** The entry's base rule was "only a queen plays
   onto a king", with the real restriction demoted to "a harsher version appears
   in some rule sets". Wikipedia states it the other way round: the rule is
   "nothing may be played on top of a King", and *"Queens may be played on top of
   Kings"* is the first item in its list of variations **to make the game
   easier**. The entry had promoted an easing and demoted the rule.
2. **Wraparound was settled by the entry when the sources disagree.** "Ranks do
   not wrap in the standard game." That is Wikipedia's strict form — but the
   other account gives the turned corner as its ordinary rule, in as many words:
   "the ace is both high and low meaning players can go around the corner". The
   paragraph now sets out the strict form, then the two easings in the order they
   loosen it, and says plainly that accounts differ about which is the game.
3. **"This is the single rule that ends most deals, and the most commonly
   relaxed one."** Two prevalence claims in one sentence; nothing ranks either.
4. **A rule was demoted to a house rule.** "A common house rule lets you go under
   par by subtracting one for each card still face down in the stock." Wikipedia
   gives that as the scoring: "If the tableau is cleared, player scores a
   negative point for every card left in the stock." The other account is silent,
   which the entry now says.
5. **Par was attributed to nobody while a source states it.** "No authority sets
   a par for this, so the targets players quote are convention rather than rule."
   Wikipedia: "a score of 45 or lower is considered par, with a score of zero or
   lower being perfect." The 45 was in the entry; what was wrong was the claim
   that nothing states it.
6. **"A total in the mid-thirties counting as a good round."** In neither source.
7. **"Under strict no-wraparound rules most deals cannot be cleared at all, so a
   nine-hole total of zero is not a realistic goal."** A statistic nobody
   measured, used to contradict the one figure a source does give — zero or below
   is the perfect round.
8. **The redeal was stated absolutely.** "There is no redeal and no shuffling of
   the waste back into the stock." Wikipedia carries an *Impossible tableaus*
   rule: a deal that cannot be cleared — all queens covered by kings, in its own
   example — may be reshuffled and dealt again. The entry now carries the
   exception with the example.
9. **The Putt Putt variant carried a prevalence claim and an unmeasured
   effect.** "Lifts the win rate dramatically, which is why nearly every computer
   version offers it as an easy mode." Nothing counts computer versions and
   nothing measures the lift. What a source does say is that it is one of the
   *common* easings, which the variant now says instead.

**And the figure repeated two of the corrected claims.** Its caption read "Ranks
do not wrap in the standard game, so a king takes only a queen and an ace takes
only a 2" — both halves being the findings above. The three rows survive
untouched, because both corner plays really are illegal in the strict form; only
the caption needed rewriting, and it now scopes itself to that form and names
Putt Putt as the version that allows them. **A corrected fact surviving in a table
or a figure is among this audit's most reliable findings.** No number is claimed
here: the running count reached six on 2026-08-11, and two later sittings each
recorded an instance without deciding whether it belonged in that count, so the
tally is no longer one anybody can add up.

**Two variations were added, both from the source's own list**, since the audit's
finding was that the entry's account of the easings was inverted and incomplete:
jokers used as wild cards, and starting with an empty waste so the player chooses
which exposed card opens it.

**Two sentences in `setup` were corrected although this sitting had not touched
them.** "Many descriptions call that build pile the foundation" and "several of
them treat turning the first card as the player's opening move" are both counts,
and each is doing prevalence work on a one-of-two: one account calls it the
foundation, the other the discard pile; one deals the opening card, the other
makes it the player's first move. Both now name what was actually seen.

**The competitive form of the patience was missing.** Both sources have it —
a deck each and compare totals, or match play counting holes up and down — and
the entry mentioned multiplayer only to warn the reader off the *other* game
called Golf. It now distinguishes the two.

## `tripeaks` — seven

1. **The wrap's provenance was invented and its effect measured by nobody.**
   "The original game allows it and nearly every computer version follows, while
   stricter rule sets stop the sequence dead at the king and lose a noticeable
   share of otherwise winnable deals by doing so." The wrap itself is confirmed
   flatly by one source — "you may 'turn the corner' during play, and play either
   a King on an Ace, or an Ace on a King" — and the **other source never mentions
   the ace at all**, in an article that otherwise sets out the rules in full.
   Neither says the original allowed it, neither counts implementations, and
   neither measures what closing it costs.
2. **The opening card was stated flatly where the two sources give different base
   rules.** The entry turns the top stock card to open the waste. One source does
   that; the other says the waste "should be empty at the beginning" and has the
   player decide "which card should go on the Waste pile first" — which the first
   source lists as a variation for a *head-start*. The entry now gives both and
   says to settle it.
3. **"Solvability with the wrap allowed is usually quoted above 90 percent."**
   The 90 percent is real — it is the inventor's own statistical analysis — but
   it is quoted for the original game, not for a rule set defined by the wrap,
   and "usually quoted" attributes it to nobody.
4. **"One exact-solver run over about a thousand deals put it near 97."** In
   neither source.
5. **"Ordinary players report winning around half their games."** In neither
   source. What the analysis actually reports is scores, not win rates: about 12
   a hand for most people, a little over 17 for stronger players, against a
   theoretical perfect average of 60. Those are the numbers that make the entry's
   own point about the gap between solvable and won, and they were missing.
6. **The strict-ranking variant ranked itself.** "Converts a fair number of
   clearable deals into dead ends, which is why almost every published version
   leaves it open" — an unmeasured effect and a count of publishers.
7. **"Commercial versions layer their own arrangements over this, commonly a
   clock, a deduction for each draw, or a penalty for cards left standing."**
   Neither source lists those. One does give a much simpler scheme — count the
   cards left on the peaks, one point each, zero wins — which the entry now
   carries, along with the source's actual point that conceding early was part of
   the original game rather than a modern addition.

**A `background` was added.** The entry said "the original game" four times
without ever saying what it was. Robert Hogue designed TriPeaks in 1989; it
reached most of its players through Windows Entertainment Pack 3 and later the
Microsoft Solitaire Collection; and the three-peak board was deliberately built
to be replaceable, with scoring rebalanced to suit. All four claims are from the
source, and the last one is why a reader meeting an unfamiliar board should not
assume it is a different game. **Two variations were also added from the same
list**: a wild card, and dealing the peaks face up.

**The arithmetic survived in both entries.** Golf's 35 tableau cards, 17 left
over and 16 after the turn; 45 strokes over nine holes as five a hole; Double
Golf's eight-by-eight leaving 40. TriPeaks' 3 + 6 + 9 + 10 = 28 with 18 face
down, 24 remaining and 23 draws; Double TriPeaks' 56 and 47; and the streak
ladder's seven-card total of 28. Not one number was wrong.
