## The source map is wrong about `forty-thieves`, and the reason it got through

**`gamerules.com/rules/forty-thieves/` is a different game.** It is a modern
2-to-4-player connecting board game credited to Charles Magri, played with a
40-card deck, coloured chips and a 6-by-6 grid, scoring for lines of four. It
shares nothing with the two-deck patience but its name.

It cleared every bar
[the source map](../specs/2026-08-11-source-map-for-the-unverified-32.md) sets:
it answers 200, its body names the game, it carries far more than 1,500
characters, and — the check added after the Spider Web incident — **it reads its
title back correctly**, as `FORTY THIEVES Game Rules - How To Play FORTY
THIEVES`. A title check catches a page that redirects to a different game. It
cannot catch a page about a genuinely different game that happens to share the
name, which is the case here. Nothing short of reading the page finds this.

The replacement second and third sources are Denexa Games and BVS Solitaire,
both already named in the entry's `sources_consulted` and neither previously
proven. `solitairecentral.com`, also listed there, no longer answers at all;
pagat has no Forty Thieves page.

## Why these two

They are the two packers among the unaudited solitaires: both build a tableau
downward, both turn on moving cards between columns, and both were picked
because the corpus pointed at them rather than because they read alike. Four
entries named **both** `klondike` and `freecell` before this sitting —
`forty-thieves`, `yukon`, and `freecell` and `spider` themselves, which were
audited together on 2026-08-11. These two were the only unaudited pair in that
set, which made them the pairing most likely to be carrying a fact those audits
had already corrected. (Both now name neither: the sentences that mentioned
FreeCell were the universal negatives at findings 4 below, and they are gone.)

**That is where the sixteenth finding came from.** Both entries carried the same
unsupported claim about what computer versions do for scoring — `yukon`'s
"Software usually bolts on the Klondike standard system", `forty-thieves`'
"computer versions that add one usually borrow the Klondike arrangement". No
source read for either entry says anything at all about how software scores
these games. The claim is plausible, it is stated twice in the same shape, and
neither entry can show it on its own. The description of Klondike's scoring is
itself accurate — it matches what `klondike`'s own audited entry sets out — but
what neither entry could support was the ranking word. Both now say "some"
rather than "usually".

The cross-check also settled half of another finding rather than raising one.
Both entries invoked *the exhaustive solver study that settled FreeCell*, and
that half is sound: `freecell`'s own audited entry puts solvability near 99.999
percent off exactly that work, so the comparison was not invented. What neither
entry could support is the other half — the claim that no such study exists for
**its** game. The reference was accurate and the negative bolted onto it was
not, which is not something either entry could have shown alone either.

## `forty-thieves` — nine

**The flagship is the win rate, where one real number was moved onto the wrong
subject and a second was invented to sit beside it.**

1. **The 1-in-10 estimate was re-attributed to skilled players.** The entry gave
   "players who plan several moves ahead ... report something closer to one deal
   in ten". One in ten is the estimate for *the game*, flatly: "The winning
   chances have been estimated as 1 in 10 games." It is not a figure for careful
   play as against careless.
2. **"Large runs of random deals played without much care come in around four
   games in a hundred."** In none of the three sources. With finding 1 it
   supported "Both numbers are honest; they are measuring different players" — a
   distinction resting on one relocated figure and one invented one.
3. **The luck-or-skill question was settled by the entry, and the sources
   disagree flatly.** The entry: "the spread between careless and careful results
   is unusually wide for a solitaire game, which is the signature of a game where
   skill genuinely bites." One account says the opposite in as many words —
   winning "is very much dependent on luck, rather than skill" — while another
   calls it a game of "strategic depth" needing players to "think several moves
   ahead". The entry now gives both and says the question is disputed. The
   comparative — *unusually* wide for a solitaire — is measured by nobody in any
   case.
4. **A universal negative.** "No exhaustive solver study has settled Forty
   Thieves the way one settled FreeCell." Unverifiable in either direction and in
   no source; the sentence now says only that the figure in circulation is an
   estimate rather than a measured ceiling, which is what a source actually
   states ("have been estimated as").
5. **The redeal was stated absolutely where accounts differ.** "There is one pass
   and no redeal ... the stock is gone for good." Two sources agree — one puts
   "No redeal" in its summary line — but the third says "When the stock is
   exhausted, the discard pile may be turned over to refresh the stock." The
   entry now gives the one-pass rule as the strict game and says accounts differ
   over a second pass.
6. **Advice contradicted by a source's own advice.** "Because promoted cards
   never come back, the standard advice about clearing cards upward as fast as
   possible is actively bad here." No source gives that as standard advice, and
   one gives close to its opposite for this very game: uncover low cards trapped
   under high ones, "especially kings", and move them to the foundations. All
   three say aces go up as they appear. The entry now says both — aces and dug-out
   low cards go up, and everything past that is weighed against the parking space
   it costs. The underlying claim that a promoted card never returns **is**
   supported, from an unexpected direction: the source's Emperor variant lists
   being able to worry cards back as its *distinguishing feature*.
7. **"Filling one thoughtlessly is the move that most often loses the deal."** A
   frequency nobody measured. What a source does say — that success typically
   depends on clearing one or more columns, and that emptying one early is the
   first thing to do — is stronger and was missing.
8. **Josephine ranked itself.** "Lifts the win rate enormously, which is why it
   is the version many people prefer to play." Nothing measures the lift and
   nothing counts the players. A source does list moving built sequences together
   among the game's *common* variations, which the variant now says instead.
9. **Rank and File demoted a rule to a majority convention.** "Most rule sets
   also allow a descending alternating-colour run to travel as a block ... while a
   few hold to the strict one-card rule." The source states it flatly as the
   variant's rule: "sequences can be moved in part and in whole." This is
   category 2's second sub-case — a rule demoted to a house rule when a source
   gives it plainly — and it is the same shape as the finding that opened the
   `golf` audit earlier the same day.

**And the `layout` caption was still carrying the corrected redeal.** It read
"the 64-card stock is turned once and never returns" — the same absolute claim
finding 5 had just removed from the rules, restated flatly one screen above
them, where a reader skimming the diagram would take it as the rule. It now
scopes itself to the strict game. **This was found by reading the rendered page
rather than the diff**, which is the sixth consecutive sitting that step has
caught something, and it is the audit's most reliable category turning up
exactly where the taxonomy says to look. No number is claimed for it: the count
of sixteen above is of statements in the rules, on the same basis the previous
record used.

**Three variants gained the odds a source states**, since the entry ranked them
by difficulty with nothing behind the ranking: Maria at about one deal in eight,
Limited at one in five, Rank and File at one in two, against roughly one in ten
for the parent. Maria also gained its second recorded name, Thirty-six Card
Tableau. Streets is left as it was: the source gives it no figure, and the
entry's reasoning about it is arithmetic rather than a claim about the world.

**A `background` was added.** The entry carried four aliases and a Napoleon in
two of them without ever explaining either. The story is a legend and both
sources that mention it hedge it — "said to be the version he probably played",
"a legend, likely untrue" — and the entry now says so rather than leaving a
reader to assume the name is a record of something. The datable facts around it
are all from one source: the attribution is at least as old as 1870, Le Cadran
appears in 1874 and points at a possible French origin, Big Forty and Forty
Thieves are American names from the 1920s, Roosevelt at San Juan first appears in
1939, and collections have gone on printing the game under the Napoleon name
more than any other.

## `yukon` — seven

1. **The win-rate paragraph was almost entirely unsourced.** "The largest
   published tally, drawn from over half a million recorded games, lands close to
   14 in 100." No source read gives any win figure for Yukon at all — not the
   tally, not the superlative, not the sample size.
2. **An explanation attached to the figure.** "Which mostly reflects that people
   abandon awkward deals rather than grind them out." Nobody measured that, and
   it was the entry's own reasoning presented as the reason for a number that is
   itself unsupported.
3. **A count of claims.** "Claims that a patient player takes the large majority
   of deals are equally common." Nothing counts claims.
4. **The same universal negative as `forty-thieves`.** "Nobody has run the kind
   of exhaustive solver study that settled FreeCell."
5. **Worrying back was ranked with nothing behind it.** "Most rule sets let you
   take a card back off a foundation ... A few rule sets forbid the move
   outright." **None of the four sources mentions taking a card off a foundation
   at all**, in either direction. The rule is kept, because deleting a plausible
   rule leaves the reader worse off, but the counts are gone: the entry now says
   the point is not settled the same way everywhere and to agree it before
   dealing, which is a hedge without a ranking.
6. **Russian Solitaire ranked itself twice.** "One of the harder one-deck games"
   and "the best-known member of the family". Nothing ranks either. The harder
   claim is now put as what it can be derived from — building in suit rather than
   in colour halves the cards that will accept any given card.
7. **"That second ending arrives more abruptly than beginners expect."** A claim
   about beginners nobody surveyed.

**A variant a source states was missing.** "Other Yukon variants remove the usual
restriction that only Kings be placed in empty tableau spaces." Since emptying a
column is the hardest thing to arrange in a game with no stock, that is the
parent's sharpest constraint, and the entry described the constraint without ever
mentioning that it is commonly lifted. Added, under a descriptive label rather
than a name, because the source gives it none. Note the same sentence is the
source of the entry's existing kings-only rule being right: the restriction is
called *usual*.

**A live name collision was missing.** Russian Solitaire is also a name for an
old one-pack non-builder and for a two-pack open packer, neither of which is the
Yukon variant. This collection has been bitten by exactly this before, on either
side of `canfield` and `klondike`, so the variant now carries the warning.

**Two supported facts the entry lacked were added**, both of which its own
argument needed: Yukon is more winnable than the Klondike deal it grows out of
without the challenge going out of it, and skill is repaid better here than
there; and the rule of thumb that once every card is face up the deal is very
nearly home. The entry's whole strategic point is about digging out the 21
face-down cards, and the plainest statement of why that matters was not in it.

## The arithmetic survived in both entries, again

Forty Thieves' 40 tableau cards in ten columns of four, 64 to the stock, 104 in
all, eight foundations because each suit is built twice; Maria's 36 dealt and 68
left; Limited's twelve columns of three. Yukon's 1, 6, 7, 8, 9, 10, 11 summing to
52, the 28-card Klondike deal plus 24, the 21 face down and the five face up on
each of columns two to seven. Every one checks out. **Not one number was wrong in
either entry** — which is now the standing result of this audit rather than a
surprise.

## Recorded, not acted on

- **`yukon`'s Alaska and Moosehide rest on nothing read.** One source names
  Alaska and describes nothing; no source mentions Moosehide at all, including
  its warning that a few rule sets attach the name to a much harsher game. Both
  are left in place with their hedges intact.
- **`yukon`'s worry-back rule**, above, is kept without support in either
  direction.
- **Neither entry's claim about computer scoring is supported**, only hedged.
- **`forty-thieves`' Streets** is described accurately but its difficulty
  reasoning rests on arithmetic rather than a source.
- **Australian Patience** is named by two sources as closely related to Yukon and
  is not carried as a variant, because no source read describes its rules. Adding
  a variant that could not be described would have been worse than omitting it.
- **`yukon`'s `figures` block came through clean**, which is worth recording
  because a corrected fact surviving in a figure is among this audit's most
  reliable findings. Its `meld` figure shows a 7♣ with a Q♥ and 6♦ riding on it
  landing on an 8♦, captioned so that only the grabbed card must fit. That is
  exactly the rule all four sources give, and the illegal row — red on red — is
  illegal for the reason stated. The `kind` is right too: a load that travels as
  one unit is not something to wrap onto another line.
- **The variants block and every caption are outside both tools**, which is why
  the finding above had to be caught by eye. `PROSE_FIELDS` is `setup`, `play`,
  `goal_and_scoring` and `background`; the originality checker reads exactly that
  list, and the `checked.prose` fingerprint covers exactly that list. So
  `variants[].description`, `layout.caption` and `figures[].caption` are neither
  checked for copied wording nor able to invalidate a stamp when they change.
  Both halves were confirmed rather than inferred: two sentences copied verbatim
  off two different source files were planted in a variant description and the
  run came back **clean**, while the same two sentences in `play` had been caught
  at 27 and 15 words minutes earlier; the validator reported 80/80 valid with
  them in place. Nothing here is wrong today — every variant this sitting
  rewrote was read against a source by hand — but the audit's own error taxonomy
  puts the variants block level with the main rules for findings, and it is the
  part of an entry no tool is looking at. Recorded, not acted on: building the
  cover is not an audit.
- **Both entries' `difficulty: easy` was checked and left.** It looks wrong
  against a game the sources call difficult and put at one deal in ten, but the
  schema defines the field as how much there is to learn before a first game, not
  how hard it is to play well. Nothing to fix, and worth writing down because the
  next reader will wonder too.

## The originality pass, run after the fact fixes

**Both entries clean.** Neither carries a finding at any tier.

**Every tool was controlled in both directions before it was trusted.** The
Wikipedia API returns an explicit `missing` marker for an invented title, and the
control came back missing with no extract while both real titles came back
present — `Forty Thieves (card game)` through a recorded redirect to *Napoleon at
St Helena*. Both articles were fetched twice and the byte counts agreed exactly
(7,724 and 2,310), which is the guard against the rate-limited 200 that once left
an empty source file and a clean report. Game Rules, Denexa and BVS were each
controlled by reading the title back rather than by status: **an invented Game
Rules path answers 404 with a 46 KB body**, so size passes it and only the title
("Page not found") names it, and the same held for the two new hosts. No source
here is hard-wrapped — across the seven files the longest line runs from 232 to
909 characters, nowhere near the seventy-column wrap that breaks the checker's
sentence splitting — so the unwrap Solitaire Laboratory needed was checked for
and not required.

**The checker itself was then controlled per entry, against every source file
separately**, by planting a sentence copied straight off each and repeating the
run. All seven came back, each attributed to the right file with both sides
printed. Three planted in `forty-thieves` surfaced as four findings — 23 words
off Wikipedia, 17 off BVS, and 12 and 11 off Denexa, where one planted sentence
was reported as two runs broken at its parenthesis. Four planted in `yukon`
surfaced as five — 38 off Wikipedia, 27 off Game Rules, 15 off BVS, 11 off
Denexa, and a 6-word overlap between the Wikipedia sentence and BVS's much
shorter statement of the same rule, which is the checker correctly finding one
plant against two sources. All were then removed and the clean run repeated.
This matters more than usual this sitting: Game Rules, Denexa and BVS had never
been proven against this tool before, and two of them were added today.

**Three claims introduced by this sitting's own corrections were caught before
the stamp.** The previous record put that failure mode at five sittings running;
this is the sixth, and it is now the most reliable finding in the whole audit.
One was a REUSE the tool caught: the new `background` opened with six words
verbatim off a source, "this is said to be the", written while paraphrasing the
Napoleon legend. Two were caught by re-reading, not by the tool. A new sentence
in `forty-thieves`' Rank and File claimed it was "much the most winnable of
these" — a ranking across five variants of which only three have figures — and
in the same breath said the colour rule gave a *shorter* reach, which is
backwards. And the replacement for `yukon`'s win-rate paragraph reproduced one
source's three clauses in that source's own order — easier than Klondike, still
a challenge, therefore skill pays — which is the failure
[`piquet` recorded](2026-08-11-words-not-facts.md): rewriting away from one
source lands on another. It was recast to run from the deal to the conclusion
instead, which is the opposite direction.

## What this pass does not establish

Nothing about the 17 entries still carrying a 2026-08-03 stamp. Where sources
disagreed — Forty Thieves' redeal, and whether the game rewards skill at all —
the entry now says so impersonally rather than picking a winner, and this record
names the disagreements. `yukon`'s Alaska and Moosehide, both entries' claims
about computer scoring, and Yukon's worry-back rule are named above as
unverified rather than checked. The originality tool cannot certify either entry
clean; the clean results are what a controlled tool finding nothing looks like,
and thorough paraphrase is exactly what it cannot see. And the map correction at
the top establishes only that one page is the wrong game — **the other 31
entries' mapped sources have not been re-checked for the same fault**, and the
bars the map applied cannot detect it.
