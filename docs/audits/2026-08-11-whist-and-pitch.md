## The shape of it

**Nine of the twenty-two were in the variants blocks** — seven of whist's
thirteen and two of pitch's nine — against thirteen in the main rules. Knock-Out
Whist alone accounted for four. The variants are a smaller body of prose than
the rules they hang off and produced nearly as many errors, which is the
handoff's point about auditing them as hard as the rules.

**Both entries got a rule backwards about who receives something.** Whist had
the wrong player dealing after the draw for partners; Knock-Out Whist had the
wrong player dealing *and* the wrong player leading. Pitch bundled a disputed
rule with an undisputed one and stated the pair flatly.

**The arithmetic survived again** — pitch's 80 pips, its 13 books from 12 cards
plus a kitty, whist's maximum of 7 trick points from 13 tricks and the honours
values are all exact. The one quantity that was wrong was in a sentence, not a
table: pitch said that at four players "nearly half the deck never appears",
when 28 of 52 cards are left out, which is rather more than half.

## `whist` — thirteen

Main rules:

1. **The draw for partners had the wrong player dealing.** The entry said the
   lowest card of all deals first. Wikipedia: "Highest card is dealer and second
   highest is their partner." Pagat and Bicycle are both silent on it, so this
   correction rests on one source and is recorded as doing so.
2. **The rule on reviewing the last trick was inverted and hedged.** The entry
   said tables vary, and that "the strict rule is that only the trick currently
   in progress is public". Wikipedia states the opposite plainly, with no
   variation attached: before the next trick starts, a player may ask to see the
   last one.
3. **"No signalling" conflated table talk with card play.** Whist's silence is a
   rule about speech; signalling through the cards is ordinary technique, and
   Wikipedia describes the standard suit preference discard as part of basic
   tactics. The entry forbade both in one breath.
4. **"American play … dropped honours altogether"** — the 7-point target is in
   both sources; neither says anything about honours being dropped there.
   Removed.
5. **Long whist "ran to 10"** — pagat says 10, Wikipedia says 9 or 10. Now says
   9 or 10.
6. **The rubber was settled on the wrong scale.** The entry said the rubber is
   decided by the difference between the two sides' point totals with the
   2-point bonus added, which reads as the trick points that win games. Those
   are a different count: a game won is worth 1, a game in which the losers took
   1 or 2 points is a double worth 2, one in which they took none is a triple
   worth 3, and the rubber bonus of 2 sits on top of *that* scale.

Variants:

7. **Knock-Out Whist was "three to seven players"** — Wikipedia says two to
   seven, in both its infobox and its text.
8. **Knock-Out Whist had the winner dealing.** The entry gave the player who
   took most tricks the next deal. The deal rotates to the left; what winning
   earns is the right to name trump.
9. **Knock-Out Whist had the winner leading.** Eldest hand leads, as in ordinary
   whist. That is the third "who goes next" error in this entry alone, counting
   the draw for partners and the deal above.
10. **The dog's life was open-ended.** The entry gave the eliminated player a
    single card "each round". It is one card in the following hand only, and the
    dog must win a trick with it to re-enter; failing, they are out.
11. **Bid Whist's kitty was demoted to a house rule.** The entry said that "in
    the usual house rule" the buried cards count as a trick for the bidding side.
    Pagat gives it as the rule: the kitty counts as a book won by the bidder's
    team, which is how a hand of 12 cards each comes to 13 books.
12. **German Whist was scored by a variation.** The entry said only the last
    thirteen tricks count, "at 1 point for each trick above six". Pagat and
    Wikipedia both say whoever takes the majority of those thirteen wins the
    hand; Wikipedia gives the above-six count explicitly as "one version of
    scoring", alongside counting all 26.
13. **The Solo Whist ladder skipped a contract**, running from solo at five to
    abundance declared at thirteen with no mention of the nine-trick abundance
    that pagat lists.

**Not resolved.** The entry's revoke penalty — three tricks, or 3 points either
way, and the revoking side barred from winning that deal — is supported by
**neither** source read. It is the traditional penalty and it has been left in
place rather than deleted, but it is stated here as unverified so the next pass
does not read the stamp as covering it.

## `pitch` — nine

1. **Minimum players was 3**; pagat says cutthroat pitch takes "two or more" and
   Wikipedia's infobox says 2–9. Now 2. The maximum was left at 7: Wikipedia's
   9 cannot be dealt six cards each from 52 cards, so its infobox contradicts
   its own deal.
2. **The cutthroat targets were invented.** The entry gave cutthroat "11 is
   usual, 7 for a quick session", twice. Pagat says the rules are the same as the
   partnership game, which puts it at 21; the 11 in Wikipedia belongs to a
   northern English *partnership* pub game. Neither source gives cutthroat a
   target of its own.
3. **"Nearly half the deck never appears"** at four players. 28 of 52 do not
   appear, which is more than half.
4. **High and Low were asserted together.** "High and Low go to whoever was
   dealt them" states as settled fact a rule the entry itself flags three
   paragraphs later as the one thing its sources disagree about.
5. **A prevalence claim on the disputed rule** — "a great many tables, most
   partnership play among them". Neither source ranks it.
6. **The `scoring_table` carried one side of the disagreement**, noting Low as
   "lowest trump dealt" with no hint that the prose calls it contested. This is
   the sixth time a corrected fact has been found still alive in a table.
7. **The minimum bid is disputed and was stated flatly.** Pagat gives two;
   Wikipedia's auction pitch allows one.
8. **What happens when everyone passes is disputed and was stated flatly.**
   Pagat has the dealer forced to bid; Wikipedia has the deal thrown in and
   dealt again.
9. **"The single most popular addition to the partnership game"**, of the draw.
   Pagat lists it first among partnership variations, which is not a ranking.

**The near-miss, which is the useful part.** Low looked like the batch's classic
finding — a variation promoted to the base rule — because pagat's base text
awards it to the side winning the trick the lowest trump falls in, while the
entry had it going to whoever was dealt it. Reading Wikipedia's basic pitch
before acting showed the reverse: Wikipedia scores Low for the original owner
and lists the trick-winner rule as a common variation. **The sources disagree
about which rule is the ordinary one, and the entry was already right to say so.**
What was wrong was the confidence around it, not the substance. That is twice in
two sittings that reading the second source has stopped a correction rather than
prompted one.

## The originality pass, run after the fact fixes

Run against the same source text, and it earned its place both times: **each
entry's only REUSE finding was introduced by a correction made minutes
earlier.** Whist's rewrite of the review-the-last-trick rule had picked up
eleven words of Wikipedia verbatim; pitch's new sentence about pagat's Low rule
had picked up seven of pagat's. Both rewritten and re-run clean.

That is nine passes running where every REUSE finding came from the fix rather
than from the original prose. Writing a correction with the source sentence in
front of you is how you end up copying it.

Four READ findings were left alone as terms of art or formulaic procedure: "four
players in two fixed partnerships", the requirement to follow suit, and pitch's
High–Low–Jack–Game in their fixed order, which is the fact being stated and
cannot be reworded.
