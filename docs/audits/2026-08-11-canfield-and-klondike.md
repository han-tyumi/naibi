## The thing they were read together for, and it came back clean

`canfield` and `klondike` sit either side of a live name collision: in North
America Canfield is the demon game and Klondike is Klondike, while older British
books call Klondike *Canfield* and the demon game *Demon Patience*. Both
entries already explain it, and the reason to take them in one sitting was that
whether the two explanations **agree with each other** cannot be checked from
inside either one.

They agree, and both match both sources. Wikipedia's Klondike article and its
Canfield article tell the same story from opposite ends and neither entry has
drifted from it. **That is the check this sitting was arranged around and it
found nothing** — worth recording precisely because a clean result from a check
designed to fail is the only kind that carries weight.

What was wrong was the history hanging off it, in `canfield`: the entry blamed
the confusion on "a pair of 1908 rule books". It was **one** 1908 collection,
George Hapgood's, which printed both games — its "Demon Patience" the game
America now calls Canfield, its "Canfield" what is now called Klondike.

## `canfield` — nine

1. **"A pair of 1908 rule books"** — one book, as above.
2. **An empty column could be filled "with anything" once the reserve is spent.**
   Both sources fill it from the waste instead. Game Rules adds the part the
   entry also missed: filling from the reserve is compulsory, filling from the
   waste is at the player's choosing.
3. **The foundation-to-tableau return was stated as a rule.** Neither source
   mentions it for Canfield at all. Left in the entry as something rule sets
   differ over and a table should settle, rather than asserted or deleted.
4. **A strategy claim that is not achievable.** The entry said a first pass
   through the stock tells you which thirteen cards the reserve holds "and
   roughly how far down each one sits". Elimination gives you the **set**; it
   cannot give you the order, which stays hidden to the end. The entry now says
   so, which is a more useful thing to tell a player than the original was.
5. **A win rate with a sample size, and no source for either.** "About 9
   percent" across "better than a hundred thousand deals" of ordinary online
   play appears in neither source. Wikipedia's 50,000-deal figure is the
   **solver's**, not players'; Game Rules says three percent. Replaced with the
   spread and a warning not to trust any single number for casual play.
6. **A solver's average was attributed to good human play.** "Around forty of
   the fifty-two is what good play averages" — Wikipedia's 39.9 cards is what
   the solver banks on an average deal, which makes it a target rather than an
   expectation. The two numbers either side of it in the same paragraph are the
   solver's and the expert's, so this one was doing real damage.
7. **The casino price was stated flatly.** Wikipedia says sources differ between
   fifty and fifty-two.
8. **"Double Canfield" and most of its detail rest on nothing read.** The
   reserve of twenty-six and the "easier rather than harder" verdict are in
   neither source. Wikipedia describes a double-pack Canfield under the name
   **Variegated Demon**, with aces fixed as the base, five tableau columns,
   single cards or sequences movable, and two redeals. The variant now says
   that.
9. **Two more, both caught by reading the rendered page rather than the diff.**
   The win-rate paragraph opened "the honest answer has three numbers in it and
   they are all correct", which stopped being true the moment the third turned
   out to have no source — a lead sentence contradicting its own paragraph three
   lines later. And the closing line had the game "named after a casino"; it is
   named for Richard Canfield the gambler, and it is his casino that never
   carried it. That is three sittings running in which the rendered page has
   found something the diff did not.

## `klondike` — seven

1. **The Thoughtful Klondike argument was inverted.** The entry had it that "any
   deal solvable in Thoughtful Klondike is solvable in Klondike too, so solving
   it gives an upper bound". That implication runs the wrong way, and if it held
   the figure would be a **lower** bound. The true direction is that any deal
   winnable blind is winnable while seeing everything, which is what makes the
   Thoughtful rate a ceiling. The entry's own conclusion was right and its reason
   for it was backwards.
2. **The foundation-return prevalence was reversed.** "Permitted in most rule
   sets ... a few forbid it outright" against Game Rules, which says the
   original Klondike does not allow it at all and only "some versions" do.
3. **Player win rates with no source.** "Near a third at draw one and around one
   game in ten at draw three", varying "from site to site", is in neither.
   Wikipedia's actual human figures are an experiment crediting a strong player
   with 43 percent at draw three and an old rule book's 1 in 30 for a single
   pass. Those are what the entry cites now.
4. **"The older and harder default"**, of draw three. Older and harder are both
   supported; **default** is not, and Game Rules presents the single card as the
   ordinary way to play.
5. **"Most rule sets let you take just the lower portion of a run."** Nothing
   ranks the two conventions.
6. **Vegas priced the deck at 52 without noting the 50.** The casino game the
   scheme copies is described at 50 in Wikipedia. Both the prose and the
   `scoring_table` note say so now — the table being the place a corrected fact
   has most often been left alive.
7. **The 82 percent figure carried a condition its source does not state.** The
   entry attached "unlimited redeals" to it in two places. Wikipedia gives the
   figure for draw three and says nothing about passes; the unlimited-passes
   phrase belongs to a different study on the same page. Removed from both.

**Recorded, not acted on.** Five of the ten rows in `klondike`'s
`scoring_table` — five for turning up a tableau card, minus fifteen for a
foundation return, minus twenty and minus one hundred for recycling, and the
floor at zero — are supported by neither source. Wikipedia gives the waste,
foundation and timed figures and stops. They have not been deleted, on the same
reasoning as `mau-mau`'s table earlier today: nothing read contradicts them. The
two-deck half of Double Klondike is in the same position; Wikipedia's "Double
Solitaire" is only the two-player game, which the entry describes correctly.

**That is now three tables in forty-eight audited entries carrying values no
source read supports**, after forty-five with none. The pattern is not
arithmetic going wrong — every number that can be checked still checks out —
it is scoring schemes borrowed from software, which the sources these entries
name do not document.
